# Volatile should rank device states, not device types

The guidance backs Volatile's core idea: tag each live device with what to do next and log every action. It does not back the Lens's current content. Every first-responder standard read in primary text (NIJ 2008, ACPO v5 2012, SWGDE 2018 and 2025, INTERPOL 2021) organises on-scene handling around **device state** (on and unlocked, on and locked, off, destructive process running, encryption mounted). None of them uses a fixed ranking of device types. Order of volatility (RFC 3227) ranks data tiers *within* a system, not objects in a room. Measured against these documents, the Lens's step lists have problems. They tell an untrained responder to "Capture RAM" and "Export connection table", both actions the documents reserve for trained staff. They treat airplane mode as reliable isolation, which the current mobile guidance (SWGDE 2025) no longer accepts. They leave out the near-universal "do not" rules: don't switch it on, don't shut it down through the OS, unplug at the device end. They also leave out the destructive-process check that NIJ, SWGDE and INTERPOL put first for a running computer. The real-world record is thin in one specific way. **No sourced study or case quantifies first-responder seizure errors**, so the motivation has to rest on documented mechanisms: encryption, the iPhone AFU-to-BFU reboot, remote wipe and Faraday leakage. It also rests on live-seizure operations like Silk Road and AlphaBay, and on the absence (in limited searching) of any product that guides the physical seizure sequence. Human-factors evidence predicts that a guided overlay will cut errors but roughly double step time. It also predicts attention tunnelling and automation bias, and that tick-box use can erase a checklist's benefit. The design therefore has to collect proof that each step was done, not just a tick, and has to keep the responder looking at the room.

## The evidence base is strong on procedure and silent on error rates

The procedural sources are solid and were read in primary text. They are RFC 3227 (2002), NIST SP 800-86 (2006), NIST SP 800-101r1 (2014), the NIJ first-responder guide (2nd ed., 2008), ACPO Good Practice Guide v5 (2012), SWGDE Best Practices for Digital Evidence Collection v1.0 (2018), SWGDE Mobile Device Evidence v2.0 (August 2025) and INTERPOL's First Responder Guidelines v7 (2021) ([RFC 3227](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3227); [NIST SP 800-86](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-86.pdf); [NIST SP 800-101r1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-101r1.pdf); [NIJ 2008](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/219941.pdf); [ACPO v5](https://training-on-eppo.era.int/EPPO/kiosk/pdf/ACPO_Good_Practice_Guide.pdf); [SWGDE Mobile 2025](https://www.swgde.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025-08-21-Best-Practices-for-Mobile-Device-Evidence-Collection-Preservation-Handling-and-Acquisition-18-F-003-2.0.pdf); [INTERPOL 2021](https://www.interpol.int/content/download/16243/file/Guidelines%20to%20Digital%20Forensics%20First%20Responders_V7.pdf)). The SWGDE 2018 collection document was read in full, but the notes give only its index page as a URL ([SWGDE documents](https://www.swgde.org/documents)). Several of these are old. No third edition of NIJ 2008 was found, no revision of NIST 800-86 was found (it still mentions floppy disks), and no successor to ACPO v5 was found. The UK forensic regulator's digital appendix has been replaced by a statutory code that was not read ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-forensic-services-codes-of-practice-for-forensic-service-providers)). ISO/IEC 27037 is paywalled, and only its table of contents was checked ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/44381.html)). The USSS seizure guide, the Council of Europe Electronic Evidence Guide v3, ENISA's first-responder guide and SWGDE "Capture of Live Systems" were not read at all.

Two canonical "orders of volatility" exist, and they measure different things. RFC 3227 §2.1 ranks storage by lifetime: **registers and cache; then routing table, ARP cache, process table, kernel statistics and memory; then temporary file systems; disk; remote logs; physical configuration and network topology; archival media** ([RFC 3227](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3227)). NIST 800-86 §5.2.1.2 ranks OS artefacts by how fast they time out, and puts **network connections and login sessions before memory contents** ([NIST SP 800-86](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-86.pdf)). ACPO and INTERPOL say to start with RAM, so that later tools don't overwrite it ([ACPO v5](https://training-on-eppo.era.int/EPPO/kiosk/pdf/ACPO_Good_Practice_Guide.pdf); [INTERPOL 2021](https://www.interpol.int/content/download/16243/file/Guidelines%20to%20Digital%20Forensics%20First%20Responders_V7.pdf)). None of the first-responder documents expects the officer to run that capture. NIJ is blunt: untrained responders should not "explore the contents of or… recover information from" a device beyond recording the screen, and should "not press any keys or click the mouse" ([NIJ 2008](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/219941.pdf)).

The peer-reviewed technical evidence is narrower but hard. **Halderman et al. (2008)**, read in full, measured DRAM losing its contents in about **2.5 to 35 seconds at operating temperature**, and recovered BitLocker, FileVault and TrueCrypt keys from memory ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/sec08/tech/full_papers/halderman/halderman_html/)). That hardware is SDRAM-to-DDR2 era. No DDR4 or DDR5 decay curve was found, so the figure shows that pulling power destroys RAM evidence quickly; it is not a current timing. Faraday-bag tests (2010–2016) found that many commercial bags leak and that the seal matters, but these were seen only as abstracts or snippets ([ECU, Lennox-Steele & Nisbet 2016](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/adf/165/); [Purdue CERIAS 2010](https://www.cerias.purdue.edu/assets/pdf/bibtex_archive/2010-27.pdf)). The iPhone timing rules come from vendors and reverse engineers, not from Apple or peer review. USB Restricted Mode cuts Lightning data after one hour locked (iOS 11.4.1/12, 2018) ([Magnet Forensics](https://magnetforensics.com/blog/ios-11-4-1-follow-up-delaying-usb-restricted-mode); [ElcomSoft](https://blog.elcomsoft.com/2018/09/ios-12-enhances-usb-restricted-mode/)). An inactivity reboot to BFU arrived in iOS 18.1 (2024); it was reported at 72 hours, and as 7 days in early iOS 18 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/14/new-apple-security-feature-reboots-iphones-after-3-days-researchers-confirm); [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2024/11/14/ios-18-3-day-iphone-reboot/)).

Some evidence was simply not found, and that matters for how Volatile is pitched. **No peer-reviewed study or sourced case counts real first-responder seizure errors.** That would mean encrypted machines powered off, phones wiped after seizure, or timestamps altered by browsing. The closest work is a 258-officer vignette survey showing that recognition of digital evidence varies with field experience ([Holt et al. 2020, abstract only](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/PIJPSM-07-2019-0119/full/html)). There is also an RTI/NIJ survey in which about 91% of agencies said they decide on-scene collection themselves ([NIJ summary, snippet only](https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/new-dna-recommendations-agencies-consider-implementing-improve-digital)). No criminal case was found in which evidence was excluded because an officer powered off or browsed a device. No named case was found in which a closed lid led to a full-disk-encryption lockout. No named corporate incident was found in which in-house reimaging destroyed evidence.

The documented cases point the other way: what decides outcomes is live access and documentation. In the Silk Road arrest, agents staged a distraction and grabbed Ross Ulbricht's open laptop before he could close or encrypt it ([Business Insider, trial testimony](https://www.businessinsider.in/the-fbi-staged-a-lovers-fight-to-catch-the-alleged-kingpin-of-the-webs-biggest-illegal-drug-marketplace/articleshow/45982727.cms)). In the AlphaBay case, Thai police seized Alexandre Cazes's laptop open and logged in. One officer said the FBI wanted "his computer online and his mobile phone unlocked", and that a locked device "could have taken around 300 years to crack". That is a single quoted estimate, not a measurement ([Bangkok Post](https://www.bangkokpost.com/thailand/general/1293243/internet-drug-king-said-to-be-worth-b10-billion)). The counterexample is Francis Rawls, who was held about four years in civil contempt over FileVault drives that investigators never opened ([Justia, 3d Cir. 2020](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca3/17-3205/17-3205-2020-02-06.html)). The 2014 UK reports of seized phones wiped remotely in police custody are trade-press re-reports of BBC FOI work; the originals were not retrieved ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2014/10/10/police_say_criminals_remotely_wiping_seized_mobes/)). In Detroit-area police, iPhones in storage rebooted after iOS 18 and became harder to unlock, which is the inactivity reboot described above ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/apple-quietly-introduced-iphone-reboot-code-which-is-locking-out-cops/)). The famous criminal "digital evidence failures" (Amero, Casey Anthony, Liam Allan) were failures of analysis and disclosure, not of seizure ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna19074272); [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/computer_expert_i_told_prosecutors_casey_anthony_didnt_do_84_chloroform_wor); [Law Gazette](https://lawgazette.co.uk/practice-points/sex-texts-and-disclosure-/5064089.article)). They support Volatile's custody log more than its triage. Courts also accept reasoning from file access times (*U.S. v. Grosenheider*), which gives "don't browse before imaging" real evidential weight ([vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/u-s-v-grosenheider-885451761)).

## Eleven device states, four live conflicts, one corporate exception

Across the documents, a few rules are close to universal. Secure the scene and keep the suspect away from devices. Photograph screens and cabling before touching anything. Never switch on a device that is off. Never shut down through the OS. Remove power at the device end, never at the wall, because a UPS may sit in between. Isolate from networks. Label every cable and port. Log every action with its time. Past that, the rules depend on state. The table gives the synthesised rule for each device and state, with its strongest source. A blank or "none found" means the notes contain no sourced first-responder step.

| Device / state | On-scene rule (synthesised) | Key sources and caveats |
|---|---|---|
| Computer on, unlocked | Photograph the screen with date, time and time zone. Check for a destructive process ("delete", "format", "wipe"); if one is running, pull power at the device end immediately. Otherwise **do not shut down or close programs**, and do not type or click. Check for encryption (BitLocker, FileVault, VeraCrypt), mounted volumes, cloud and webmail sessions, and remote access. If any are present, keep it live and **call trained personnel** for RAM and logical capture. **If no trigger is present, the guides split.** NIJ (pp. 25–26, flow chart) and ACPO (step 13) lead to removing power at the device end and the laptop battery, with ACPO's standby caveat. INTERPOL says don't turn it off, but to turn it off if no expert can be obtained. Decide on network isolation by balancing remote tampering against losing open connections. | INTERPOL §3.4, §5.3; ACPO steps 8–13; SWGDE 2018 §6; NIJ pp. 25–26. INTERPOL alone tells responders to disable screensavers and screen lock, which conflicts with NIJ/ACPO "don't touch". |
| Computer on, locked | Photograph it. A short mouse move is allowed only to reveal a password prompt, with the time recorded. Then stop touching it. Ask the user for the password and record it, treating the user's advice with caution. Restarting to bypass the lock destroys volatile data. Cold boot or powered transport to the lab is **expert-only**. If encryption or valuable live state is suspected, keep power on and escalate. If not, the same split applies as for unlocked: NIJ/ACPO lead to removing power at the device end; INTERPOL prefers keeping it on where an expert is available. | ACPO steps 9–13, 16; INTERPOL §3.4.2; NIST 800-86 §5.4; NIJ pp. 25–26 |
| Computer off | **Do not switch it on**, under any circumstance. Check standby mode by moving the mouse, since standby is a running system. Remember that some laptops power on when the lid opens. Photograph and label cables and ports, remove power at the device end and the laptop battery if possible, tape the power switch, and record make, model and serial. If RAID is suspected, seize the whole machine. | NIJ p. 24–25; ACPO steps 5–7; SWGDE 2018 §6; INTERPOL §3.4(b), §5.3(b). Battery removal is often impossible on modern laptops (INTERPOL §5.4). |
| Server / networked business system | **STOP: do not turn it off.** Request staff trained in network seizure. Weigh the cost to the business (damages claims), and whether the administrator can be trusted. Prefer a selective logical copy plus event logs, Active Directory, mailboxes and backups. | NIJ p. 27 and flow chart p. 29; ACPO p. 31; INTERPOL §5.2 |
| Router / network device | **No source gives a first-responder step list.** NIJ says to record network and wireless access points, because they show evidence may exist beyond the scene. ACPO says configuration can be viewed through admin screens, but that doing so adds data (a Principle 1 breach) and needs credentials and prior research into the device's logging. RFC 3227 places routing and ARP state in the same tier as memory. "Photograph lights and cabling, leave powered, escalate" is a reasonable inference, not a quoted rule. | NIJ p. 20; ACPO Appendix p. 25; RFC 3227 §2.1 |
| Phone on, unlocked | Keep it on, **keep the screen active and connect power**. Extend auto-lock if trained to, or keep interacting with it. Check MDM and parental controls before changing settings. Cover cameras and microphone. Isolate it, and **verify** that it is isolated: airplane mode may leave Bluetooth, BLE and Wi-Fi on. In a Faraday enclosure, the power source goes inside and **no cable crosses the shield**. Be aware of on-body detection, trusted places and trusted-watch auto-lock. Photograph smear patterns. Move it to acquisition at once. | SWGDE 2025 §5.3.1–5.3.3; INTERPOL §5.1.3–5.1.4 |
| Phone on, locked | Do not guess passcodes, since limited attempts and dead-man switches exist. Connect power so it does not reboot. Isolate it with RF shielding. Move it to acquisition immediately. A reboot moves it from AFU to BFU, and "some data unrecoverable". | SWGDE 2025 §3, §5.3.4–5.3.5; [RMRCFL 2023](https://www.rcfl.gov/rocky-mountain/documents-forms/rmrcfl-byte-sized-tips-2023.pdf) (search summary only) |
| Phone off | **Leave it off. Do not apply power**, because some phones boot when charged. Do not power a damaged phone. A phone found submerged stays submerged in the same liquid. Record visible identifiers (model, IMEI, carrier). Isolate it and transport it. Do not remove the SIM or memory card on scene. | SWGDE 2025 §5.4; INTERPOL §5.1; NIST 800-101r1 §4.3 |
| Removable media (USB, SD, external disk) | Photograph it in place, label the media and the port it occupies, then disconnect. On a live machine, the live-capture decision comes first. Do not trust write-protect tabs; any triage goes through a write blocker. No adhesive labels on discs. Package in anti-static or tamper-evident packaging. A card inside a phone stays in the phone; adoptable storage is encrypted to that device. | NIJ p. 24; INTERPOL §5.5; ACPO p. 33; SWGDE 2025 §3; NIST 800-101r1 |
| IoT / smart home / wearables | Smartwatch: treat it as a phone (power, isolation, Faraday). It may be what keeps the phone unlocked. Smart TV: check for standby, video any UI interaction, read the manual first. Smart speaker: seize only if there is reason to think it holds data; unplug and seize as found. IP camera: look for a memory card, since footage may be in the cloud. Novel devices: consult experts. | INTERPOL §5.7; SWGDE 2018 §6; SWGDE 2025 §3, §5.3.1. Research places most smart-speaker data in the cloud and companion apps, not the device ([arXiv 2408.15768, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.15768)). |
| Encrypted volume mounted on a live machine | This is the clearest "do not power off" trigger. Live or logical acquisition is "necessary", because a physical image of the disk would be unreadable. Expert only. | INTERPOL §4.2(a), §5.3(a); NIJ p. 21, 26; ACPO step 10 |

The conflicts are not footnotes; they are where a tool has to make a choice and show it. The biggest is the shift in the computer default. NIJ 2008 calls removing power "generally the safest option", and it gives a Windows-specific reason: unplugging preserves last-login and recently-used data ([NIJ 2008](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/219941.pdf)). INTERPOL 2021 says "If the equipment is on, do not turn it off" ([INTERPOL 2021](https://www.interpol.int/content/download/16243/file/Guidelines%20to%20Digital%20Forensics%20First%20Responders_V7.pdf)). ACPO already noted in 2012 that live collection "has become a common practice", even though it changes data ([ACPO v5](https://training-on-eppo.era.int/EPPO/kiosk/pdf/ACPO_Good_Practice_Guide.pdf)). Even INTERPOL falls back to "better to turn off" when no expert is available, so "never power down" is not a universal rule either.

Shutdown method conflicts too. NIST 800-86 prefers a graceful OS shutdown by default. ACPO forbids it because it may trigger wiping software. Both agree that wall-socket unplugging is wrong ([NIST SP 800-86](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-86.pdf)). Phone advice has reversed. ACPO 2012 said power phones off. NIJ 2008 said leave them as found. SWGDE 2025 and INTERPOL say keep them on and charged ([SWGDE Mobile 2025](https://www.swgde.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025-08-21-Best-Practices-for-Mobile-Device-Evidence-Collection-Preservation-Handling-and-Acquisition-18-F-003-2.0.pdf)). Airplane mode has gone from accepted, with a GPS caveat (NIST 2014), to first choice with Faraday as fallback (INTERPOL 2021), to "may not disable" radios, so verify or shield instead (SWGDE 2025). Smaller conflicts remain. NIJ bans plastic packaging, while ACPO allows aerated plastic bags. NIJ says don't move a computer until it is off, while INTERPOL describes powered transport as a specialist option.

Corporate ransomware response inverts part of the criminal logic. CISA, FBI, NSA and MS-ISAC guidance (2023; seen through search summaries rather than read in full, so check the PDF before quoting) says to **isolate first**, at the switch level if needed. It says to power down "if—and only if" isolation is impossible, because shutdown loses volatile-memory artefacts. It also says to capture memory and images from a sample of affected devices ([CISA Ransomware Guide](https://www.cisa.gov/stopransomware/ransomware-guide)). Criminal guidance instead treats network isolation as a balance. ACPO warns that cutting the network loses "the list of currently open connections". INTERPOL keeps cloud sessions open only where legal authority covers them. In a business, the network administrator may be a helper or a suspect, and seizure can create damages liability (ACPO, INTERPOL §5.2). Practitioner accounts from Volexity and Microsoft IR name panicked shutdowns, reboots and reimaging as the classic corporate mistake ([Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/cyberattacks-data-breaches/what-not-to-do-in-a-cyberattack); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/03/10/incident_response_advice/)). Sophos found telemetry missing in more than 42% of 232 IR cases ([Sophos 2023](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/press-releases/2023/11/cybercriminals-disabled-or-wiped-out-logs-82-attacks-missing-telemetry)). Volatile should therefore ask "criminal seizure or incident response?" before it shows any network step.

## Five of Volatile's twelve steps are wrong, outdated or unsupported

The table below checks each current step against the guidance. "Wrong" means the guidance contradicts the step or reserves the action for experts. "Outdated" means newer guidance supersedes it. "Unsupported" means no source backs it. "Incomplete" means the step is right but missing parts the guidance requires.

| Current step | Verdict | Why, and the replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Laptop 1. Photograph screen | Supported, incomplete | Universal. Add "include date, time and time zone" (INTERPOL), add a written note of screen content (ACPO step 8), and do not touch the keyboard or mouse. |
| Laptop 2. Capture RAM | **Wrong for a first responder** | Every first-responder guide gates live capture to trained staff (NIJ p. ix; ACPO Principle 2, "must be competent"; SWGDE 2018 "consult more experienced personnel"). Replace with "Do not shut down. Call trained personnel for live/RAM capture", triggered by encryption, relevant screen content, or remote sessions. Keep "RAM first" as the expert's note (ACPO, INTERPOL). |
| Laptop 3. Note open sessions and network connections | Partly supported | INTERPOL §5.3 has responders check mounted volumes, cloud and webmail sessions and network connections, by observation. Reading connection tables needs typing commands, which NIJ/ACPO forbid. Reword to "Observe and photograph visible sessions, cloud sync and network links; don't type." |
| Laptop 4. Isolate from network | Contested, wrongly placed | ACPO makes this a judgement call (remote tampering vs losing open connections). CISA puts isolation *first* in ransomware response. As the last of four steps it is wrong for both scenarios. Branch it by scenario, and in criminal mode make it a deliberate decision. |
| Laptop, missing | Gap | The destructive-process check, which comes first: pull power at the device end if it is wiping. The encryption check. Don't shut down via the OS. Don't close the lid (an inference from INTERPOL's prevent-suspend step and the Silk Road seizure, not a quoted rule). Unplug at the device, not the wall. Standby caveat before removing the battery. Label cables. Timestamp every action. |
| Router 1. Photograph status lights and cabling | Supported by inference | Consistent with NIJ "record network and wireless access points" and the general cabling-photo rules. No router-specific source exists. |
| Router 2. Export connection table | **Wrong / unsupported** | It needs admin login, which ACPO says alters data (a Principle 1 issue) and needs credentials and research into the device's logging. No first-responder source tells officers to do this. Replace with "Do not log in. Record make and model and visible SSIDs; flag for an expert." |
| Router 3. Leave powered on | Unsupported (reasonable inference) | No document says this for routers. It follows from RFC 3227 placing routing and ARP state at memory-level volatility and from NIJ's server rule. Label it as inferred, and pair it with "escalate". |
| Phone 1. Photograph screen | Supported, incomplete | Add recording the device clock against a reference clock (NIST 800-101r1), battery, signal and lock state (SWGDE 4.1), and photographing smear patterns (SWGDE 5.1). |
| Phone 2. Airplane mode | **Outdated** | SWGDE 2025: airplane mode "may not disable Bluetooth, BLE, Wi-Fi… or may only disconnect them temporarily". It also needs touching the phone, a risk NIST flagged. Replace with "Isolate: RF enclosure preferred; if airplane mode is used, manually confirm the radios are off." |
| Phone 3. Faraday bag | Supported, incomplete | Add a power bank inside the bag, **no cable crossing the shield**, and a check that the bag works: commercial bags often leak (Lennox-Steele 2016, abstract only). |
| Phone, missing (and "screen on" conflates states) | Gap | "Screen on" mixes up unlocked and locked, and the two need different actions. Unlocked: keep the screen awake, connect power, cover the cameras, check MDM. Locked: no passcode guesses. Both: move to acquisition immediately (SWGDE 5.3.3.4). Off: leave off, no charger. |
| USB 1. Photograph in place | Supported | NIJ p. 24 and INTERPOL. Add "label the port it occupies". |
| USB 2. Bag and label | Supported, incomplete | Add: if the USB drive is in a live machine, don't remove it before the live-capture decision (a mounted encrypted container may sit on it, by inference from INTERPOL §5.3). Use anti-static or tamper-evident packaging. Never test it on scene without a write blocker. |

Five steps have substantive problems. Two are wrong for a first responder (Capture RAM, Export connection table), one is outdated (airplane mode), one is unsupported (router "Leave powered on"), and one is contested and misplaced (Isolate as laptop step 4). A sixth, "Note open sessions and network connections", needs rewording so it means observing, not typing. Router step 1 holds as a reasonable inference. The other five (laptop step 1, phone steps 1 and 3, both USB steps) are correct but incomplete. The laptop list is missing the destructive-process check, which comes first in NIJ, SWGDE and INTERPOL. The phone list is missing its nearest equivalent, watching for the anti-forensics and auto-lock signs SWGDE 2025 lists.

The fixed rank order (laptop #1, router #2, phone #3, USB #4) has no source. RFC 3227's ladder applies inside one system. It puts router routing and ARP tables and computer memory in the *same* tier, so it cannot place laptop above router. Phone guidance carries the most explicit clocks: on-body auto-lock when the phone is set down, an inactivity reboot to BFU, and USB Restricted Mode at one hour ([SWGDE Mobile 2025](https://www.swgde.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025-08-21-Best-Practices-for-Mobile-Device-Evidence-Collection-Preservation-Handling-and-Acquisition-18-F-003-2.0.pdf); [Magnet Forensics](https://magnetforensics.com/blog/ios-11-4-1-follow-up-delaying-usb-restricted-mode)). An on, unlocked phone can therefore be the most time-critical object in the room, and the AlphaBay team planned around keeping exactly that phone unlocked. A defensible replacement is a **state-derived urgency** score, which is my synthesis and not a published rule. In descending order, the tiers are: any device with a visible destructive process; on/unlocked devices that can lock or sleep (phone or computer), with encryption or mounted volumes raising the priority; on/locked phones, because of reboot and wipe risk; servers and routers, which are "leave on, escalate" rather than "act now"; and powered-off devices and removable media, which are stable. The Lens should show *why* a device ranks where it does, so the ranking reads as reasoning rather than authority.

## No product found guides the physical seizure, and AR guidance carries known costs

The commercial on-scene market is mature but starts at the plug. The main products are Magnet RESPONSE, a free collector that grabs RAM first, then logs and registry; Magnet OUTRIDER, a triage scanner focused on illicit content; ADF's DEI, Triage-G2 and MDI suites; Cellebrite's Field Solutions and Kiosk; and MSAB's XRY Tablet and Kiosk ([Magnet RESPONSE](https://www.magnetforensics.com/resources/magnet-response/); [OUTRIDER](https://www.magnetforensics.com/products/magnet-outrider/); [ADF](https://www.adfsolutions.com/triage-g2-pro); [Cellebrite Field Solutions](https://www.officer.com/investigations/forensics/digital-forensics/product/21143455/cellebrite-digital-intelligence-data-forensics-for-investigations-cellebrite-field-solutions); [MSAB frontline](https://www.msab.com/roles/frontline-personnel/)). All of these capabilities are vendor-described. The independent check is NIST CFTT, which tested the XRY and UFED kiosks ([NIST CFTT](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ssd/software-quality-group/computer-forensics-tool-testing-program-cftt/cftt-technical/mobile)). Every one of them works on one device after someone has decided to connect it. Custody systems (Axon Evidence, Tracker Products SAFE) begin at evidence intake, with barcodes, RFID and hash-verified audit trails ([Axon G-Cloud listing](https://www.applytosupply.digitalmarketplace.service.gov.uk/g-cloud-14/services/235501581421077); [Tracker SAFE](https://www.prweb.com/releases/Digital_Evidence_Management_Streamlined_with_New_Tracker_Products_SAFE_and_Axon_Evidence_com_Integration/prweb17944490.htm)). Cellebrite's 2025 survey reports that 60% of examiners' agencies store digital evidence mainly on internal servers, home-grown systems or USB sticks (vendor survey) ([Cellebrite 2025](https://cellebrite.com/en/industry-trends-survey-2025/)). Police AR work exists. Examples are Black Marble's HoloLens scene-documentation proof of concept (2017), TU Delft/TNO remote-expert and evidence-tagging AR trialled with Dutch police and NFI (2012–2016), and Vuzix Blade patrol pilots (2020). All of it targets physical scenes, not device seizure ([Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/devices/2017/10/09/black-marble-uses-microsoft-hololens-help-revolutionise-crime-scene-investigation-tuserv/); [DutchNews](https://dutchnews.nl/2016/11/dutch-police-trial-crime-scene-augmented-reality-system); [TNO "ART of CSI"](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-39420-1_35.pdf); [Vuzix](https://ir.vuzix.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1809/netherlands-national-police-unit-using-vuzix-blade)).

The gap Volatile fills is the **minutes between entering the room and plugging anything in**. That is when someone decides which device to touch first, records its state, and starts the custody record. The research found no product, AR or otherwise, that covers that window. The search was limited, and many vendors (Detego, Belkasoft, Exterro, Oxygen, Paraben) were not researched, so the claim is "none found", not "none exists". The case for that window rests on several sources: training lasts days, not weeks (FLETC's basic course is 3 days) ([FLETC](https://www.fletc.gov/basic-incident-response-digital-evidence)); the UK inspectorate found more than 25,000 devices queued, with waits of up to 18 months ([HMICFRS 2022](https://hmicfrs.justiceinspectorates.gov.uk/publication-html/how-well-the-police-and-other-agencies-use-digital-forensics-in-their-investigations)); and Cellebrite's 2026 survey says 56% of devices arrive locked (vendor) ([Forensic Focus](https://www.forensicfocus.com/news/cellebrites-2026-industry-trends-report-reveals-smartphones-as-the-leading-source-of-digital-evidence-in-investigations-at-97/)). A scene mistake cannot be fixed by an overloaded lab later.

Human-factors evidence supports guidance on the work piece, at a price. In the one smart-glasses RCT read in full, 31 paramedics using a triage algorithm on glasses reached **92% accuracy versus 58% without, but median time per triage rose from 16.6 s to 37.0 s**. Major errors occurred only in the control group, and Wi-Fi failures removed 10 of 362 triages ([Follmann et al. 2019, JMIR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682285/)). The classic finding that overlaid AR instructions cut assembly errors by 82% was seen only as an abstract ([Tang et al. 2003](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/comparative-effectiveness-of-augmented-reality-in-object-assembly/)). A HoloLens assembly study found paper faster, HoloLens less error-prone, and paper lower in perceived load (snippet only) ([Kolla et al. 2021](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/47824/1/SSRN_CLF2021_Kolla_V3_Final.pdf)).

The risks are just as concrete. In HUD studies, pilots reacted to an unexpected runway incursion about 2.5 s later when symbology was head-up. NASA researchers attribute this "cognitive tunnelling" to superimposed, non-conformal symbology (secondary summary) ([NASA, Foyle et al.](https://human-factors.arc.nasa.gov/groups/HCSL/publications/Foyle_AvPsyc01.pdf)). In an AR-HMD search study, the most effective cues produced the most **automation bias**: users followed cues that were wrong 17% of the time (abstract only) ([Warden et al. 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2303.14300)). Checklists can also fail by tick-box. The WHO surgical checklist pilot cut deaths from 1.5% to 0.8% ([Haynes et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/)). A mandated rollout across 101 Ontario hospitals, with near-100% reported compliance, showed no significant effect, and commentators read that as perfunctory use (abstract and commentary) ([Urbach et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620866/)).

For hands-busy input, gaze-dwell triggers Midas-touch errors. Dwell times of about 600 ms suit younger users and about 800 ms older users (snippet only) ([Kyushu](https://catalog.lib.kyushu-u.ac.jp/opac_download_md/7153269/7153269.pdf)). Voice confirmation of gaze is one published workaround ([EyeTAP, preprint](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2002.08455)). No study was found that measured gloved pinch accuracy on AR glasses. For custody, blockchain proposals abound but lack legal analysis ([Robertson 2024](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/bitstreams/6a3d005b-957e-4f9b-bbbb-a6589d9b798d/download)), and no study evaluated voice or wearable custody logs in court.

Taken together, these findings set five design constraints. Keep the central view clear and put text at the edges. Make the responder look up regularly ("trace cables; scan for devices the Lens has not tagged", ACPO step 18). Make every tag visibly "responder-confirmed" rather than machine-asserted. Require proof of completion (a photo or a stated observation) rather than a tick. Run fully offline.

The video under 3 minutes should show one room with four stand-ins whose ranking changes as states are set. A natural sequence is a phone set to "on, unlocked" jumping to #1 with its clock running, then a laptop flagged "encryption visible" turning into "do not power off, call expert". ## Conclusion

The research turns Volatile from a ranked device list into a decision aid. Its value is not in knowing that phones go in Faraday bags. It is in asking the two or three state questions that decide whether the right move is "leave it on", "pull it now" or "don't touch, call someone", and then proving afterwards what was done and when. That reframing also answers the strongest objection to an AR checklist. Tick-box compliance and automation bias are failures of a tool that asserts. A tool that asks, shows its sources and conflicts, and records evidence of each action sits on the side of the literature where guidance helped.

Two honest limits should shape the pitch. First, the field has no measured baseline of first-responder error, so Volatile cannot claim to fix a quantified problem. It can claim to encode current guidance, some of it newer than the 2008–2012 documents still in circulation, at the point of action. Second, the guidance itself is old and split. Software that dates and cites each rule can be updated when SWGDE or Apple changes the ground, which no laminated card can do. That updatability is the most defensible long-term argument for building this as software at all.
